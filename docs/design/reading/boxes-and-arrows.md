## What it is

Boxes and Arrows is a foundational online journal dedicated to the practice,
innovation, and theory of information architecture (IA), interaction design, and
user research. Founded in 2001 by Christina Wodtke and later stewarded by
volunteer editors including Amy Jiménez Márquez, it established much of the
vocabulary and methodologies used in modern digital product design.

Rather than focusing on ephemeral visual trends or CSS tricks, Boxes and Arrows
examines how digital spaces are organized, classified, and navigated. Its
extensive back-catalogue covers topics such as card sorting, faceted search
architectures, mental models, user flow diagrams, content strategy, and
deliverables like site maps and wireframes.

In July 2022, the publication entered an indefinite hiatus. However, its entire
20-year library remains permanently preserved and freely readable online as an
essential historical and practical archive for information architects and
complex systems designers.

## Paywall and pricing

- **Free:** All articles, essays, case studies, and diagrams published across
  the journal's history are 100% free to read without payment or paywalls.
- **Paid:** None. There are no paid tiers, memberships, or paywalled articles.
- **Sign-up required?** No. Accessing the entire publication archive is
  completely open without registration or authentication.
- **Licence for client work:** Articles and diagrams are copyrighted by their
  individual contributors and the journal. The methodologies, taxonomies, and IA
  classification frameworks documented on the site can be freely adopted and
  implemented in commercial client projects.

Verified on the official Boxes and Arrows website and archives on 2026-10-07.

## How to use

1. Structure site navigation schemes: Search the archive for foundational IA
   essays—such as Donna Spencer's "Four Modes of Seeking Information"—when
   architecting navigation hierarchies and search filters for large content
   sites.
2. Design complex data classification: Reference articles on taxonomies,
   thesauri, and faceted navigation when modeling relational content schemas in
   headless CMS setups (e.g., Sanity, Strapi).
3. Plan user research deliverables: Read practical guides on conducting
   effective card sorting, tree testing, and cognitive walkthroughs to validate
   navigation schemes with actual users.
4. Establish clear system mental models: Use the diagramming and wireframing
   conventions documented in the journal when crafting system architecture
   diagrams for clients and engineering teams.

## Examples

- [Four Modes of Seeking Information and How to Design for Them](https://boxesandarrows.com/four-modes-of-seeking-information-and-how-to-design-for-them/)
  — Donna Spencer's landmark framework analyzing known-item, exploratory,
  re-finding, and comparison searching behaviors.
- [Information Architecture for Audio: Doing It Right](https://boxesandarrows.com/information-architecture-for-audio-doing-it-right/)
  — In-depth guide detailing how to structure non-textual, linear media assets.
- [Better Content Management through Information Architecture](https://boxesandarrows.com/better-content-management-through-information-architecture/)
  — Methodological breakdown connecting CMS schema design with user-facing
  navigation.

## When to use it

- Use it when designing large-scale intranets, documentation portals, complex
  e-commerce catalogs, or multifaceted enterprise SaaS applications.
- Use it to learn classical information architecture principles that remain
  relevant regardless of frontend framework churn.
- Skip it when seeking modern component animation tutorials or rapid CSS tricks;
  consult [Codrops](../reading/codrops.md) or
  [Smashing Magazine](../reading/smashing-magazine.md) instead.

## Agent notes

- Non-machine-readable: The site does not provide an API, CLI, or `llms.txt`.
- The site is on indefinite hiatus (no new articles published since mid-2022),
  but the static WordPress archive remains online, fast, and fully readable.
- Useful for agents to retrieve proven structural frameworks when asked to
  design site trees, navigation menus, or CMS schema hierarchies.
